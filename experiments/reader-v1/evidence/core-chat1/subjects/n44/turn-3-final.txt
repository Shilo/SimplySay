Use the **original for the display** because its stored pixels provide enough resolution at the requested print size.

The requirement is at least **240 image pixels per printed inch**, or 240 PPI. Multiply each print dimension by that requirement:

- Width: **10 inches × 240 PPI = 2400 pixels**
- Height: **7.5 inches × 240 PPI = 1800 pixels**

So the image needs at least **2400 × 1800 pixels** for this print. The photo and print area are both 4:3, so their proportions match.

To calculate what each copy actually provides, divide its pixel dimensions by the print dimensions:

| Copy | Stored pixels | Horizontal density | Vertical density | Meets 240 PPI? |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Original | 4000 × 3000 | 4000 ÷ 10 = **400 PPI** | 3000 ÷ 7.5 = **400 PPI** | Yes |
| Web copy | 1000 × 750 | 1000 ÷ 10 = **100 PPI** | 750 ÷ 7.5 = **100 PPI** | No |

The “dpi” label doesn’t change these calculations when the print size is fixed.

These editing operations have different effects:

- **Changing only the resolution label:** With resampling disabled, changing 300 to 600 leaves the web copy at 1000 × 750 pixels. It changes the suggested print size: at 600 PPI, that copy corresponds to about 1.67 × 1.25 inches. If you print it at 10 × 7.5 inches, it still provides 100 PPI.
- **Reducing pixel dimensions through resampling:** Software combines or discards pixel information. That is how the original became the web copy. It helps meet website size limits, but leaves less detail available for later enlargement.
- **Enlarging pixel dimensions through resampling:** Software creates additional pixels by estimating their values. Enlarging the web copy to 2400 × 1800 would give it enough pixels numerically, but would not restore the original’s captured detail or establish equivalent print quality.
- **Changing physical print size without resampling:** The pixel count stays fixed. A larger print spreads those pixels over more inches and lowers PPI; a smaller print raises PPI.

For a later **10 × 8 inch borderless layout**, check proportions before resizing. That layout is **5:4**, while the photograph is **4:3**. The choices are:

| Choice | Result |
|---|---|
| **Crop to fill 10 × 8** | The photograph fills the area, with some content removed from the left and/or right. |
| **Preserve the entire photograph** | It fits at 10 × 7.5 inches, leaving 0.5 inch of unused height—typically 0.25 inch above and below. This needs margins or a designed background. |
| **Stretch to 10 × 8** | The proportions change and subjects become relatively taller. This requires an explicit decision because it distorts the photograph. |

For the crop option, the original can become **3750 × 3000 pixels**: remove 250 pixels of width, split between the sides according to the composition. At 10 × 8 inches, that supplies **375 PPI** in both directions. The minimum for that layout is **2400 × 1920 pixels**.

Keep the original unchanged and make separate exports for each job. These calculations establish resolution suitability; judging sharpness, compression artifacts, and finished-print appearance still requires inspecting the image and a sample print.
